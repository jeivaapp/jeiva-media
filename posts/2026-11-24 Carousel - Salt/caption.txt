Salt is woven into how India eats: dal tadka, pickles, papad, namkeen with chai. So it's not surprising that most of us eat more than we realise.

The study behind these numbers, part of India's National NCD Monitoring Survey, measured salt through urine samples rather than asking people to guess. That makes it a more reliable picture of what we actually eat. Men averaged more than women, but both were well above the WHO's suggested limit.

Why does this matter? Your body needs some sodium, but too much makes it hold on to extra fluid, which raises blood pressure over time. High blood pressure, in turn, is one of the biggest drivers of heart attacks and strokes, and it often has no symptoms.

What's surprising is how little of it comes from the salt shaker alone. The same survey found that about 1 in 3 adults ate salty snacks at least once a month, and many ate high-salt home-cooked food every week. Pickles, papads, chutneys, sauces and ready mixes add up quickly.

Cutting back slowly, a little less in each dish, is an easy place to start. The WHO also suggests lower-sodium salt substitutes as an option, but they aren't right for everyone, especially people with kidney problems, so check with your doctor first.

Small swaps, every meal.
