Where did 10,000 steps come from? It caught on as a catchy, round number, not because a study set it.

So researchers went looking for real answers. They pooled data from 15 groups of adults, nearly 47,500 people who wore step counters and were then followed for an average of seven years.

What they found is good news for anyone who's ever felt they "failed" at 10,000.

Benefits started well below that number and grew steadily with each extra step. People in the most active group, averaging close to 11,000 steps a day, were about half as likely to die during the study as those in the least active group, who averaged around 3,500.

And the gains levelled off sooner than many expect, especially for older adults.

Pace mattered less than you might think. Once total steps were taken into account, walking faster added little extra. In other words, the steps themselves are what count.

Ways to add steps without a gym: a walk after meals, taking calls on your feet, stairs instead of the lift, or getting off one stop early.

Start where you are. Add a little. Keep going.

Source: Paluch et al., Daily steps and all cause mortality, Lancet Public Health 2022. For information only, talk to your doctor about your own health.
